First item you need to know is keep the oil sight glass full to just slightly above the window. That is 2.5 liters just like Kawasaki states. Check it every gas stop. Reason for this is running the freeway puts you at or over 5000 rpm and this thing will eat oil then... enough that if you run low on a trip you will burn the engine right up. Wish someone had made that clear to me in first week I had the bike. It's a quirk of this engine. You can ride forever at and above 5000 rpm, but gotta watch the oil at every gas stop and add. Under 5000 rpm she doesn't eat oil so much.


Know that one item right away and stick to it. Everything else you have time to learn about, but won't need to if you don't keep the oil level up.


Oil change every 1500 miles. Doohickey adjust every other oil change.


Items for your future are doohickey, clutch safety switch, sidestand safety switch, fuel petcock, the seat hurts, footpegs slippery when wet, secret fuel reserve, subframe bolts, by the time you know those, you'll be aware of the rest that are good to know.


You said you wanted this one to be your last bike. This engine often makes it to 80K miles and more.... very likely it will indeed outlive your wish, (if you check your oil!).

Wii changes is at 1500... there's some who change at 2k... others who change at 2500, some who change at 3000, and a few (full synthetic) who insist it's ok out to 5000.


I change "around" 2500. Oil filter change every other. Doo adjust every other (or so).


The debate could rage on for PAGES with some quoting oil analysis, others quoting manufacturer recommendations...


You could change your oil every 100 miles if you really wanted to... but everyone agrees, the longer between oil changes, the less effective your oil becomes, and the greater likelihood of damage to your moving bits.


Pick an interval that you're comfortable with.


Change your filter every other time.


Adjust your doo around every 5k.


Don't sweat it.

KLRs are fun bikes to ride and are a tremendous value, but they do have some issues you have to work around. There are essentially two generations of bikes to consider, they are very similar and a lot of parts are common to both, but there are differences in several issues. Gen 1 bikes are '07 and older, Gen 2 are '08 and newer.


The doohickey is the harmonic balancer chain tensioner. This is designed to let you take up the slack in the balancer chain by loosening a small bolt on the left side of the engine, tapping it, then re-tightening the bolt. Unfortunately Kawasaki must have given design responsibility for this to a dumber than usual monkey because what they came up with to be generous sucks rocks. When the doohickey is no longer working your engine vibration increases greatly.


On Gen 1 bikes, the doohickey lever is a welded part that is known to fragment resulting is hard bits of metal traveling through the working parts of your engine (a very not good thing). In addition the tensioner spring used Gen 1 bikes tends to break (leading to no further tensioning of the chain until the spring is replaced). The broken spring happened to my '07 (somewhere prior to 7400 miles). Here is a pic of my very own doohickey.


Here is one that grenaded (thankfully not mine):


In Gen 2 models Kawasaki replaced the grenade known as the doohickey lever with a single piece part that no longer disintegrates. Unfortunately it is a very sloppy fit on the shaft which offends the mechanical perfectionist in me. They also fixed the breaking spring problem by the simple expedient of installing a longer spring. Sadly the longer spring runs out of adjustment tension somewhere around 5000 miles (basically just long enough to get out of warranty).


Luckily the KLR community has benefitted greatly by a number of dedicated people who analyzed the root cause of this and came up with an excellent solution. The correct solution is to order a doohickey upgrade kit from Eager Mike and install it posthaste. Once done, you no longer have any reason to be concerned about this for the life of your motorcycle.

Regarding oil. Understand that motorcycles put a lot more stress on oil than a car engine does. The oil in a bike lubricates the engine, the wet clutch and the transmission. The clutch and transmission shear the viscosity additives in oil a lot faster than a car engine engine does. This means that 15W45 weight oil is looking a lot more like 15W oil after 3000 miles


In addition, the piston rings in a KLR are pretty lousy resulting in lots of combustion products getting into the oil (your oil looks filthy really fast after an oil change). The lousy piston rings are a big part of the reason why KLRs tends to burn oil above 5000 RPM. The only solution I know of to burning oil is to install an oversized piston with higher quality rings (a $500 solution). I plan on installing a 685 kit when mine gets a lot more miles on it and starts excessive oil burning. For now I just check my oil every time I fill the gas tank.


Other upgrade issues to keep in mind.


The subframe mounting bolts on the Gen 1 KLRs are made from partially congealed oatmeal. If you actually intend to ride off road or weigh more than 100 lb it is probably a very good idea to upgrade these to harder, stronger bolts. I am a very big man (6'4", 265 lb) so I did the drill through upgrade which replaces the two upper bolts with a single longer bolt that goes all the way through.

Eaglemike

http://www.eaglemike.com/Subframe-bolt-upgrade-SUBO.htm

Eaglemike

http://www.eaglemike.com/Drill-through-subframe-upgrade-kit-DTSUK.htm


Gen 2 KLRs have larger diameter and slightly stronger mounting bolts so just replacing them with harder/stronger bolts is typically enough (although if you are big boy or ride aggressively the drill through option is still recommended).


If you limit your riding to street, then do the doohickey and add a set of crash bars and you are OK on the farkle front (although farkling never really ends). The crash bars are a really good idea even for street riding because the KLR is really tall and pretty top heavy. It is very easy to drop one when getting on and off the bike, especially if on an uneven surface. A cracked side cover will just ruin your day. I try to drop mine on alternate sides so the crash bars don't end up looking lopsided. You have probably seen this, but I think it is both accurate and hilarious so here goes.

http://youtu.be/Zibj7KnjClk


If you want to go off-road then a metal skid plate and a low profile oil drain plug are considered mandatory. The stock skid plate is made of plastic and is really only a gentle suggestion to rocks in the trail to not wreck your engine. The stock oil drain plug sticks out past the skid plate resulting in a perfectly predictable catastrophe if you ever manage to hit it with a rock.


This is what it can end up looking like when the stock oil plug and stock skid plate meets rock (pretty much worse case, but still).


Other minor issues:


Single piston engines like the one in a KLR create high levels of vibration. This means that bolts loosening up during a ride are perfectly normal. Any time you remove a bolt you need to re-tighten it to the specified torque. A bit of blue loctite is also generally indicated.


Screws used in Gen 1 KLRs require JIS spec screw drivers. A normal Phillips driver will remove it, but will strip out the head on reinstallation. This effect is enhanced by the somewhat solidified oatmeal nature of KLR hardware. I have replaced approximately 30% of the hardware on my KLR (basically if it is not a hex head bolt I replace it if when remove it). I wish I had realized this when I first bought the bike. I could have saved a lot of money and trips to the hardware store if I had just ordered a big box of 6mm screws when I bought the bike.


Don't let this list scare you. All of these (with the exception of the oversized piston) are inexpensive and simple to deal with. I find a lot of satisfaction in doing all my own maintenance and upgrades. I have installed numerous other upgrades beyond those described and plan for more November 1st. Everybody needs a hobby and mine seems to revolve around riding and improving my KLR. I wouldn't have it any other way.
